Live, measured, and reproducible from the repository

Millions of card payments an hour, each decided in milliseconds before the money moves. What happens when the fraud changes?

When you tap a card, the bank has a few dozen milliseconds to approve the payment, send it to a person to check, or decline it. Deciding that fast is the easier problem. The harder one is that fraud keeps changing, so a model that was right last month starts making mistakes, usually because the traffic it learned from no longer looks like the traffic it sees. This platform is the machinery around the model. It decides every transaction in milliseconds, notices when the traffic shifts, retrains, and does not promote a new model until a person has reviewed the evidence.

- to decide 99 transactions in every 100 at 1,000 a second on the live machine, against a 50 ms budget
- transactions an hour, and it kept up four times the live rate, one scorer, every run
Same day every fraud shift flagged on the day it began and nothing flagged in the clean week before the first one
- more fraud caught a year by the same eight analysts by ranking the review queue on expected loss instead of score

The live stream is synthetic. A generator plays card transactions over an entity graph of 200,000 cards, 150,000 devices and 4,000 merchants, with three kinds of fraud acting on it, and no real payment data is involved. A public fraud competition's data runs through the same pipeline offline. Every number on this page says which of the two it came from and carries a 95 percent confidence interval. See how it's built in four diagrams.

What happens in those few milliseconds

Each transaction goes through the same five steps. The times are the 99th percentile of each step, measured on the live machine with the live configuration: the champion model deciding, a challenger scoring beside it in shadow, and every decision written to history. Pick a load to see where the time goes as it rises.

    The model is the smallest part.

    The sixteen features the model sees, in plain words

    A feature is a fact about the recent past, such as how busy this card has been or how many different cards this device has carried. Each one is computed once, by one piece of code, and written in the same call to the store training reads and the store serving reads, so the model is never trained on a number it will not be given in production. A test recomputes them from the raw log and fails if any of them could have seen the future.

    How fast, and how much it can take

    Five runs at each load, on the live machine (4 virtual CPUs). The lines are the typical decision (p50), the slowest one in twenty (p95) and the slowest one in a hundred (p99), timed from the moment a transaction is sent to the moment its decision is on the stream. The whiskers are the 95 percent interval across runs. Point at a load to read it.

    A load test times every decision exactly, but only for twenty seconds. Running for days, the platform shares the machine with its own housekeeping, and the dashboard estimates its percentiles from histogram buckets instead of timing each decision, so its p99 reads higher. The next two sections are measured that way: three days of rehearsal before go-live, then the live window day by day.

    What happens when the machine is taken away

    The platform was built to run on a spot instance: spare cloud capacity at a fraction of the price, which AWS can take back with two minutes' notice. For 72 hours before go-live the whole stack ran at the live rate, and AWS took the machine away - times. Each mark below is one of them, and its length is how long the platform took to decide on time again.

    Transactions wait in the stream while a new machine boots. The scorer saves its feature state to a disk that survives the machine, so a replacement restores what every card, device and merchant has been doing and does not treat them all as strangers for a day. The minutes of catch-up count against uptime and are reported beside the latency.

    Spot did not survive the live window. The window opened on a 16 GB spot machine on 28 September, and in its first 28 hours AWS took the machine back thirteen times. The platform recovered by itself from twelve. The thirteenth exposed two faults, a restore that ran the machine out of memory and state files left empty by the hard stop, and the window was stopped. It restarted on 29 September for thirty days on an on-demand machine with 32 GB, which AWS does not take back. The repository's ADR 31 has the details.

    The live window, day by day

    Every decision since the live window opened, counted from the metrics the stack kept. Each bar is one UTC day, and its height is the share of that day's decisions that took longer than the 50 ms budget, from the moment a transaction was sent to the moment its decision was on the stream. A bar below the dashed line is a day whose p99 was inside the budget. The thirty days ended on 29 October; the stack runs on after that so the last week's fraud labels can arrive, and those days are drawn too. Point at a day to read it.

    Every day as a table

    Fraud changes. Does anyone notice?

    Fifty days of generated stream, 86 million transactions, with the fraud changing three times on days the monitors were not told about. Each bar is one day: how many of the monitored quantities moved past a fixed threshold, compared with the week the model was trained on. The monitors watch the model's own score as well as its inputs.

    Every change is flagged on the day it starts, and the quiet week flags nothing. The second shift matters most. Amounts move while the share of fraud stays the same, so an alarm on the fraud rate would see nothing, yet the model is now scoring traffic unlike anything it was trained on. The thresholds are published conventions, fixed before any drift was seen and not revisited after these results.

    How a day is judged, and why two days are needed

    What retraining can and cannot do about it

    The alarm opens a retraining request. The job fits a new candidate, compares it with the current champion on transactions neither has seen, writes the evidence into a pull request, and stops. The score is PR-AUC, how well a model puts fraud above everything else; 1.0 is perfect, and 0.03 is what guessing would score on this stream.

    Nothing promotes itself

    1. 1Drift on two days running opens a request, and an email says so.
    2. 2A candidate is fitted once enough of the new days' labels have arrived, and compared on rows neither model saw.
    3. 3A pull request carries the model file and its evidence. Merging it is the approval to run it in shadow.
    4. 4A week in shadow: it scores live traffic beside the champion and decides nothing. Or, sooner, a replay: it scores recorded days it was never trained on, from the features the scorer served, beside the scores the champion gave live.
    5. 5The promotion gate needs its whole confidence interval to clear a non-inferiority margin, then opens a second pull request.
    6. 6A person flips the flag. Rolling back is the same flag: - from the flip to the old champion deciding again.

    During the live window the platform opens these pull requests itself, on the repository's pull requests page, where anyone can read the evidence on the day it is written.

    The first live model was wrong from the first day

    The champion that opened the window had been trained on a scaled-down copy of the stream, 4,000 cards and 80 merchants. Live, among 200,000 cards and 4,000 merchants, its inputs took values it had never seen, and it flagged far more transactions than it should while catching little more fraud than chance. Nothing caught this at first. The drift monitors compare the stream with its own first days, so a model that is wrong from day one looks normal to them, and the labels that say which transactions were fraud arrive a week late. Once the labels were in, the platform measured the model, retrained on the window's own history, and judged the new model on days it had never seen. A person approved it on 12 October.

    Then the fraud shifted

    The retrained model decided for ten days. From 10 October the monitors flagged a shift in the average amounts per card, device and merchant, and a second candidate, fitted on the shift's first two days, went through the same replay and the same person.

    The monitors have flagged the stream again since, most recently on 27 and 28 October, in the same amount averages and in how many cards a device carries in a day. A candidate for that shift needs the labels for 29 October, which arrive about 6 November, hours before the stack stops, so it may not reach the gate in time. Any pull request it opens appears on the repository's pull requests page.

    Who should the analysts look at first?

    The bug it caught on the first day

    A costly mistake in fraud modelling is training on information the model will not have at decision time. Such a model scores well in testing and badly in production. The test for it was written a week before the first feature existed, and it failed on the day the features were written.

    12:00:00.000payment A
    12:00:00.000payment B sees A in its own past
    12:05:00.000payment C

    Two payments on one card can carry the same timestamp. The engine counted the first as history when it scored the second, which a model in production could never know at that instant. On the public competition data it served a wrong value on 65 of 590,540 rows, about one in nine thousand: too rare for any accuracy figure to show, and permanent once trained on.

    How to read the live dashboard

    The dashboard is the platform's own monitoring, public and read-only. It is built for the person running the system, so here is what each part shows and what normal looks like.

    The six numbers across the top

    The median, p95 and p99 of event to decision over the last 30 days and the last 7, each drawn over a trend of the same figure. A number turns red while it is over the 50 ms budget. On 31 October the 30-day p99 was 46.6 ms and the 7-day p99 49.4 ms.

    Decisions a second

    Between about 775 and 1,450 in the window's last week. The traffic follows a daily cycle, quietest around 08:00 UTC and busiest in the evening, with some noise each hour, as real card traffic has.

    Event to decision, p99, last five minutes

    The latency right now, estimated from histogram buckets. Over the last week it read about 25 ms at the quietest times and about 46 ms at the middle of the range, and it passed the 50 ms budget in the busiest evening hours, when the history compactor competes with the scorer for four virtual CPUs. The load test reads 8 ms because it times each decision exactly with nothing else running.

    A gap, then a burst

    A scorer restart, usually to roll out a new image. Decisions stop and "since the last decision" climbs for five or six minutes while the scorer restores its state, then it works off the backlog faster than it arrives. Every transaction is still decided, and the late ones count as late. On the spot machine before 29 September, the same shape meant AWS had taken the machine back.

    While caught up

    The same latency with every catch-up left out, which shows how the platform performs when it is current. The live report leaves out only catch-ups after a reclaim AWS announced, and says how many.

    Scorer hops

    The scorer's own work, split into features, model, rules and writing the decision. Each is well under a millisecond, so a hop that climbs points to a regression in the code.

    Model deciding

    Which model version made the decisions. It changes only when a person promotes or rolls back a model, and each change is a merged pull request you can read.

    Sealed before it started

    A drift monitor tuned to the shifts it will face proves nothing. So the live window's fraud schedule (when each shift comes and what it does) is derived from a secret sealed before the window opened. Its fingerprints were committed to the repository, and the secret is kept where the platform's builder cannot read it. It is published the day after the stack stops, about 7 November, so anyone can check that the schedule never changed.

    What this page does not show

    • The live window's full report is not written yet. The thirty days ended on 29 October, and the stack runs on until about 6 November so the last week's fraud labels arrive. The window's latency, uptime, drift alarms and retraining go into the README's tables after that. Until then the dashboard and the day-by-day chart above are the live record.
    • The stream is synthetic, and how hard it is depends on its size. On the scaled-down copy the first champion was trained and tested on offline (4,000 cards, 80 merchants), it scored - PR-AUC. On the full live stream of 200,000 cards and 4,000 merchants the same sixteen features separate fraud far less well: -. Guessing would score about 0.03. On the public competition data, using only the card and amount features a stream could compute, with a median of one transaction per card, the offline champion scores -. None of these says how well fraud can be caught in general; the stream exists to exercise the platform.
    • The neural challenger loses on both tracks, by an interval that excludes zero, so the promotion gate refuses it on both.
    • One region, one machine, one scorer. The architecture decision records say what would change at ten times the scale. None of it is built.
    • The review queue's dollars depend on stated prices (the cost of a review, the share of fraud recovered anyway). Which ranking catches more holds when the prices change; the dollar amounts do not.
